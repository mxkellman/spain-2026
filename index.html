Gaudí, and two workshops with your hands in things. A train across the middle of Spain, a day in Toledo, and a dinner you cook yourselves.

Part one · three nights

Barcelona

Barcelona is two cities stitched together at Plaça de Catalunya, where you are staying: the medieval old town running downhill to the sea, and the Eixample, a nineteenth-century grid of wide avenues running uphill, where the rich of 1900 had Gaudí and his rivals build their extraordinary houses. Three days: a quiet arrival; a Sunday that follows Gaudí's broken-tile mosaic from a house front to a chocolate workshop to the bench at Park Güell; the Sagrada Família on Monday; and your own mosaic before Tuesday's train.

Three nights · 21–24 November

Where you are staying in Barcelona

Catalonia Plaza Catalunya, Saturday 21 – Tuesday 24 November. Carrer de Bergara 11 is a one-block street off the west side of Plaça de Catalunya, between Ronda de la Universitat and Carrer de Pelai. The Catalunya metro entrances (L1 red, L3 green) are two minutes away on the square, and the Aerobús stops on its far side.

Part two · three nights

Madrid

Madrid became Spain's capital in 1561 and grew around the royal palace. Its oldest streets — Madrid de los Austrias, named for the Habsburg kings — run between the palace and the Plaza Mayor, and your hotel is at their edge. You arrive on Tuesday evening with the Christmas lights on; Wednesday is Toledo; Thursday is one museum, a rest, and dinner cooked with your niece; Friday is a slow morning and the airport.

Three nights · 24–27 November

Where you are staying in Madrid

Urban Hive Madrid, Tuesday 24 – Friday 27 November. Cuesta de Santo Domingo 9, a short sloping street between the Plaza de Santo Domingo and the Ópera. Santo Domingo metro (L2 red) and Ópera (L2 red, L5 green) are each about three minutes away; the Royal Palace is a 6–8 minute walk.

    Tickets and booking numbers are on your phones, not here. Keep them downloaded rather than in an email or behind a link — you will be scanning in at the top of a hill and inside a stone basilica, and signal is not guaranteed at either.

    Renfe: booking the trains

    Renfe is Spain's national railway, and it runs both trains still to book: the AVE from Barcelona to Madrid on Tuesday, and the Avant from Madrid to Toledo on Wednesday. Book on renfe.com (it has an English version) or in the Renfe app. Tickets come as QR codes to show at the gate.

    The AVE. She needs her own ticket and seat; under-14s get 40% off. The Elige fare gives you one free change and a 70% refund if you cancel; Básico is cheapest but cannot be refunded. The AVE includes up to three bags each, which is why it beats Ouigo, iryo and Avlo with three suitcases. Prices rise as the date gets closer.

    The Avant to Toledo. Book a return, about 9:00 out and 19:00 back. Every seat is reserved. If you change your mind, the fare cancels for 15% — cancel both legs in one go, because cancelling a single leg of a return costs 40%.

    Passports, and which ID to carry

    Sagrada Família, Monday. Its rules ask for a national ID card or passport, and your tickets are in the names you booked with — so bring all three passports, including hers. A US driver's licence is not on its list. Staff often only scan the barcode, but it is not worth finding out.

    Park Güell, Sunday. The tickets are named and staff may ask for ID. Bring the passports that day too.

    Every other day. The US State Department advises leaving passports in the hotel safe and carrying a photocopy or phone photo of the photo page. Spanish law expects foreigners to be able to identify themselves, so keep that copy on you.

    Metro or taxi, for three of you

    She pays full fare on both metros. Barcelona only lets under-4s ride free; in Madrid free travel ends at 6.

    Barcelona. A single ticket is €2.90, so one journey for three is €8.70. A T-familiar (€11.50 for eight journeys, shareable, plus €1 for the card) brings that to about €4.30 for the three of you. A T-casual is cheaper per trip (€13 for ten) but it is one person's card, so you would need three. A taxi across the centre is about €9–13 on weekdays and €10–15 at weekends and after 20:00. The airport is €35–40 by taxi against about €22 for three on the Aerobús.

    Madrid. A single ticket is €1.50–2.00. The 10-trip ticket (€7.30, plus €2.50 for the card) can be shared — tap it once per person — so a journey for three is about €2.20. A taxi across the centre is about €7–11 by day, a little more at night. The airport is a flat €33 by taxi.

    So: for three people, a short taxi ride costs about the same as three single metro tickets. With a shared card, though, the metro is roughly a third of the price in Barcelona and a quarter in Madrid. What to buy: two T-familiar cards in Barcelona (sixteen journeys between you) and one or two 10-trip tickets in Madrid, from the machines in any station. Take taxis with luggage, up the hill to Park Güell, and home from late evenings.

    Madrid airport: the €33 rule

    A taxi between Barajas and anywhere inside the M-30 ring road — your hotel included — is a flat €33, day or night, with no supplements.

    Allow three hours before a transatlantic departure. T4 and T4S are a separate terminal from T1–T3, so check yours in the airline app the night before.

    Dinner at nine, and how to cope

    Spanish kitchens open for dinner at 20:00–20:30, and 21:00 is normal. Make lunch the big meal — the €13–20 menú del día — and bridge the gap with a merienda of churros and hot chocolate around 5:30.

    The trick that works with a child is to eat at a counter, not a table. Barcelona's tapas houses — Ciudad Condal, Cervecería Catalana, Tapas 24 — serve straight through the afternoon, so at 7:00 you walk in, while at 9:15 it is a forty-minute wait. In Madrid, Casa Labra starts dinner at 18:00, and the Mercado de San Miguel serves all day.
